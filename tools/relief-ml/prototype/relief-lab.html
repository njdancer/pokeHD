00The question

Can a small network look at a Game Boy sprite and tell which way each pixel's surface faces? If it can, every tile and Pokémon gets 3D relief straight from the ROM, with nothing hand-made.

Nobody has labelled the game's art with directions, so we make our own training data: random 3D objects, drawn in the game's style, where we know the right answer for every pixel.

01What the model outputs

For every pixel, a direction: the way that bit of surface faces. Pictures show a direction as a colour, so a whole sprite's answer becomes a colour picture called a normal map.

Green: faces up (floors, table tops) Blue-violet: faces you (walls, fronts) Pink: faces right Teal: faces left Pale blue-green: a roof sloping back

The ball shows every direction at once: its top is green, its middle faces you, and its sides turn left and right.

02Where the training data comes from

A script builds random scenes of boxes, houses, shelves, trees, tables, stairs and boulders. It draws them in the game's 4 shades, with outlines, dither and drop shadows, and records the true direction of every pixel. Each pair below is a drawn sprite and its answer.

The drawing style was tuned against the real maps first. Real Game Boy art lights fronts almost as brightly as tops, and only uses black for outlines. Our first renders didn't do that, and the first model learned the wrong habits.

03How we measure error

Practice test (made-up scenes). Here we know the exact answer, so we measure the angle between the true direction and the model's guess, averaged over pixels. 0° is perfect and 90° means facing completely the wrong way. We hold back 5% of the scenes the model never trains on, and score those.

Real test (the actual game). Nobody knows the exact shapes in the game, but our tile labels say roughly: floors and grass face up, walls and shelves face you, roofs slope at 45°. For each pixel we check which of those three the model's guess is closest to, and count how often it's right. Floors are most of every map, so we average the three kinds equally.

Two yardsticks: chance (33%) and shade alone, the best you could do by looking only at each pixel's shade and ignoring its surroundings. Beating shade alone means the model is using shape, not just brightness.
